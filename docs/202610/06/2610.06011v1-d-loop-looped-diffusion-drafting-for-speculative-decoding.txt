## ABSTRACT 

Block diffusion accelerates speculative decoding by drafting multiple tokens in one forward pass. However, each position predicts a marginal distribution without observing earlier proposed tokens, limiting draft quality and acceptance length. We identify a concrete failure, the repetition trap , in which neighboring positions produce redundant copies of the same token. We explain this tendency theoreti-cally and empirically examine its association with shorter accepted drafts. Recent methods refine marginal predictions with an additional causal head or a separately trained drafter, increasing parameter storage and introducing separate training ob-jectives. We instead propose D-Loop, which introduces intra-block causal condi-tioning within the original diffusion drafter without additional model components. Inspired by semi-autoregressive generation and parameter sharing, D-Loop reuses the same backbone across looped passes. The first pass proposes a block, and the second conditions on a selected prefix to regenerate the suffix in parallel. A complementary prefix–suffix objective trains the shared drafter for both anchor-only prefix prediction and prefix-conditioned suffix prediction. Across eight math, code, and chat benchmarks, D-Loop can beat DFlash and DSpark on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B with obvious gains. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models generate outputs through a sequence of autoregressive forward passes. Spec-ulative decoding reduces this cost by using a lightweight draft model ( a.k.a. drafter) to propose several tokens and a stronger target model to verify them in parallel (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Its effectiveness depends on both the cost of producing a proposal and the length of the prefix that survives verification. Compared to a small autoregressive drafter, diffusion drafting offers an appealing way to reduce proposal latency. As a pioneer, DFlash (Chen et al., 2026), inspired by diffusion language models (DLMs), conditions a lightweight block diffusion model on target-model features and predicts a block of masked tokens in one forward pass. This parallel computation permits a deeper drafter than is typically affordable in token-by-token speculation. However, its central modeling limitation is the lack of intra-block causal conditioning , i.e., each position pre-dicts a marginal distribution without observing the tokens realized earlier in the same block, causing compromised proposal quality and limiting the accepted draft length. In this paper, we identify a concrete failure of marginal modeling that we call the repetition trap ,where neighboring positions produce redundant copies of the same token within a draft block. In Section 2, we provide a theoretical explanation of why marginal predictions can overproduce such repetitions and quantify their prevalence in actual draft outputs. These findings highlight the need to improve marginal draft predictions by accounting for dependencies among proposed tokens. To this end, several recent methods improve marginal draft predictions by introducing additional refinement modules. For example, DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026) augments a parallel drafter with lightweight causal heads or refiners, while D 2SD (Zhang et al., 2026) introduces a separately trained diffusion drafter for prefix-conditioned redrafting. These approaches improve draft quality but re-quire additional model parameters. Frameworks such as DSpark also combine multiple training 1arXiv:2610.06011v1 [cs.CL] 5 Oct 2026 objectives. This raises a simple question: can we alleviate the limitations of marginal modeling within the original drafter, without introducing any additional components? Such a design would be attractive for memory-constrained deployment by avoiding the storage of a separate refinement module or a second set of drafter weights. It also motivates a simpler training formulation that supports refinement without auxiliary head-specific objectives. Notably, an well-established approach to strengthening generative quality of DLMs is semi-autoregressive generation, where each block conditions on previously generated blocks while retain-ing parallel prediction within a block (Arriola et al., 2025). Such an inter-block causal conditioning is devised without adding a separate model component. Drawing on this principle and parameter sharing in recurrent-depth models (Geiping et al., 2025), we propose D-Loop, a looped diffusion drafter that operates on discrete proposal states. The core idea of D-Loop is to introduce intra-block causal conditioning through looped shared-parameter forward passes. An earlier pass proposes a token block, and a later pass conditions on a selected prefix to regenerate the suffix in parallel. Both passes reuse the full drafter with the same parameters. Our preliminary experiments found that the vanilla full-masking loss adopted by DFlash and DSpark is insufficient to enable looped forward passes. To make one drafter effective at both initial proposal and prefix-conditioned refinement, we develop a complementary prefix–suffix training objective . An anchor-only view supervises the prefix, and a revealed-prefix view supervises the suffix. Both views update shared parameters under one normalized objective. Despite an additional looped forward pass with a double drafting time (see Table 6), D-Loop still achieves better speedup performance (see Tables 3 and 4) due to its increased acceptance length compared to DFlash and DSpark. Our contributions are threefold: • We identify a repetition trap in block diffusion drafting for speculative decoding and provide a theoretical explanation of how marginal modeling can produce it. We quantify repetition in actual draft outputs and examine its association with shorter accepted drafts. • Without adding a causal head or a second drafter, we propose D-Loop, which introduces intra-block causal conditioning within the diffusion drafter itself through shared-parameter looped passes. We train both passes with a complementary prefix–suffix objective .• Experiments on math, code, and chat tasks with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B demonstrate D-Loop’s effectiveness across model scales and task domains.

## 3 D-L OOP : L OOPED DIFFUSION DRAFTING 

Overall. Instead of using any extra causal head, we propose D-Loop, which leverages intra-block causal conditioning within the diffusion drafter itself. An initial forward pass instantiates a provi-sional token prefix. Then, a second pass uses that prefix to regenerate the suffix through the same backbone. The loop operates on discrete proposal states and reuses DFlash’s target-feature condi-tioning and token prediction interface. Parameter reuse versus a new sequential head. DSpark supplies realized-token dependencies through a separate Markov or RNN head (Cheng et al., 2026). D-Loop instead invokes its existing backbone again on a different visible state. This shares the motivation of recurrent-depth computa-tion (Geiping et al., 2025), but does not recur over a continuous hidden state or assume arbitrary-depth extrapolation. Models and Evaluations. We conduct experiments with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) as target models. For each benchmark, we report average acceptance length ( τ ) and end-to-end decoding speedup over the vanilla autoregressive baseline. The quantity α = τ denotes the average tokens committed per 6cycle. Tdraft and Tverify are the corresponding average drafting and verification times per cycle. For D-Loop, costs include both draft passes, branch preparation, verification, and state updates. 

Datasets. We train the drafter on PerfectBlend (Xu et al., 2024) prompts with responses regener-ated by the target model to align the training continuations with its output distribution. We evaluate on eight benchmarks covering three domains. Mathematical reasoning includes GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), and AIME25 (OpenCompass, 2025). Code gen-eration includes HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), MBPP (Austin et al., 2021), and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024). Conversational tasks include MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) and Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023). 

Implementation. By following (Chen et al., 2026), we use a five-layer DFlash drafter with a block size of B = 16 . For Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, the drafters are initialized from Chen et al. (2026)’s pretrained checkpoints. To be fair, D-Loop and all baselines are re-trained with 5 epochs using an initial learning rate of 2e-4, and the evaluation is conducted using the final checkpoint with a batch size of 1. We set β = 0 .7 and the training loss decay scales to τs = γ = 7 . At inference, both passes share the same drafter weights, and we select K = 4 candidate prefixes for suffix regeneration. More details can be found in Appendix A.3. 

Baselines. We compare D-Loop with vanilla autoregressive decoding and three representative speculative decoding methods: EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025), DFlash (Chen et al., 2026), and DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026). EAGLE-3 uses autoregressive drafting with target-model features. DFlash gen-erates a block in one parallel diffusion pass. DSpark adds a sequential head to a parallel diffusion drafter to introduce token dependencies. These baselines cover autoregressive, parallel, and semi-autoregressive drafting. We also report a separate comparison with published D 2SD results (Zhang et al., 2026). D2SD uses DFlash for the initial proposal and a separately trained variable-prefix drafter for suffix regeneration. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

Speculative drafting and refinement. Speculative decoding verifies inexpensive proposals with the target (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025) uses target features for autoregressive drafting, while DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) predicts blocks in parallel. DSpark (Cheng et al., 2026) and Domino (Huang et al., 2026) add lightweight causal modules that condition corrections on preceding tokens. xPress (Wang et al., 2026) performs this refinement through paral-lel Jacobi updates. These methods assign causal refinement to an additional module. D 2SD (Zhang et al., 2026) selects candidate prefixes using token confidence and regenerates their suffixes with a separate variable-prefix drafter. It also evaluates direct DFlash reuse. D-Loop trains one shared backbone for both initial proposal and redrafting through complementary prefix–suffix supervision, exposing the proposed prefix to the full drafter during refinement. 

Dependencies in parallel generation. Non-autoregressive translation studies repetition and multi-modality errors (Gu et al., 2018; Ran et al., 2020). We characterize repetition in speculative drafts, where early rejection also discards the suffix. Block diffusion (Arriola et al., 2025) conditions on preceding blocks while retaining parallel prediction within each block. D-Loop applies this prin-ciple within a speculative block. Recurrent-depth models (Geiping et al., 2025) share parameters across latent computation steps. Our loop conditions on discrete proposal tokens, adding computa-tion without a second set of drafter weights. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We identify and analyze a repetition trap in marginal diffusion drafting. D-Loop introduces intra-block causal conditioning by reusing one drafter for initial proposal and prefix-conditioned suffix generation, supported by complementary prefix–suffix training. Experiments on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B show longer accepted drafts and improved decoding speed across math, code, and chat tasks. These results support improving draft dependencies without a separate refinement model. 

Limitations and future work. Our fixed looped schedule may be inefficient when acceptance is much short. On greedy Alpaca with Qwen3-4B, the acceptance gain does not offset the extra pass, slightly reducing speedup. Future work could adapt the number of passes to expected acceptance gains and latency, skipping refinement when its return is low and adding passes when worthwhile. 9REFERENCES

Deterministic drafting at both temperatures. Both draft passes use position-wise argmax at 

T = 0 and T = 1 . Prefix selection also remains deterministic. We compute first-pass token confidences from the unscaled draft softmax and select the top-K prefix lengths using the score in Section 3.1. Here top-K ranks prefix lengths, not vocabulary tokens. For the same context and model state, the initial proposal, selected prefixes, and regenerated suffixes are therefore identical at both temperatures. During a drafting–verification cycle, temperature affects only the target’s token selection. 

Greedy decoding. Let g(C) be the target’s next-token argmax with a deterministic tie-breaking rule. A branch y1: m is verified by comparing yk to g(C, y <k ). Before its first disagreement, induc-tion gives that every yk equals the corresponding token of the target’s greedy continuation. There-fore all branches’ accepted prefixes are prefixes of the same target continuation, and choosing the longest one cannot change that continuation. At its next position, a target correction or bonus to-ken also equals the next target token. Repeating the argument cycle by cycle proves equality of the complete greedy output, subject to correct branch-specific causal attention, cache rollback, and numerical evaluation of the target.

Preservation of the target distribution. At each step, the emitted token follows pT (· | h) for the actual output history h. By induction, the generated sequence has the same distribution as autoregressive target sampling at the same temperature without vocabulary filtering. Deterministic proposal and prefix selection affect how far cached verification can continue, but do not change this conditional distribution. This argument requires causal branch isolation, consistent position indices, correct KV-cache updates, and one shared target draw per position across surviving branches. 

Effect on acceptance length. For a fixed history h and proposal token v, stochastic matching occurs with probability pT (v | h). With multiple surviving branches, the probability of continu-ing verification is the total target probability mass of their distinct next-token proposals. A token accepted under greedy verification can therefore be rejected under sampling, while a non-argmax proposal can be accepted. Once a sampled token differs from the greedy continuation, subsequent cycles also receive different contexts and may produce different proposals. The observed reduction in acceptance length at T = 1 thus reflects stochastic target matching and diverging generation histories, rather than randomness in the drafter.
