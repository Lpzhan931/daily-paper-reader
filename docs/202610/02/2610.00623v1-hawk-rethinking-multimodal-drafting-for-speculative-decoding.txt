## ABSTRACT 

Speculative decoding has achieved substantial lossless speedups for LLMs, but re-mains less effective for large vision-language models (LVLMs), where lightweight drafters struggle to use rich multimodal information. A second limitation is that standard distillation supervises the drafter only along the original training trajectory, without modeling how target predictions shift after the drafter’s own proposals. As drafting moves away from this trajectory, the drafter can increasingly disagree with the target, reducing acceptance in later steps. We propose HAWK to address both limitations. HAWK uses representation similarity to select informative target layers and learns how to combine their hidden states. For visual information, it directly provides the drafter with compressed visual hidden states from the target model instead of raw visual tokens, making the visual information easier for a shallow drafter to use. HAWK also trains the drafter to capture how target pre-dictions change after its own proposals, improving its agreement with the target during multi-step drafting. On SmolVLM-256M across ten multimodal bench-marks, HAWK raises average acceptance length from 3.32 to 4.08 and speedup from 2.19 × to 2.60 × over EAGLE-3 under greedy decoding, and from 2.89 to 3.41 and 1.92 × to 2.19 × under sampling. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance across many tasks, but autoregres-sive generation remains expensive because each output token requires a sequential model forward pass (Ouyang et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Leviathan et al., 2023). Speculative decoding reduces this cost by using a lightweight drafter to propose multiple tokens that are verified in parallel by the target model (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Recent methods improve the trade-off between drafting cost and target agreement through auxiliary prediction heads (Cai et al., 2024), early-exit drafting (Elhoushi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), or lightweight drafters conditioned on target hidden states (Li et al., 2024a;b; 2025). Extending speculative decoding to large vision-language models (LVLMs) is more difficult because lightweight drafters must also use rich visual context. Existing methods therefore reduce the visual input through token compression, adaptive selection, or by reusing visual information already encoded by the target (Kang et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2026). However, reducing the number of visual tokens does not necessarily make their information easier for a shallow drafter to use, and aggressive compression may discard useful information. H AWK instead gives the drafter the visual hidden states the target computes during prefill, so the drafter starts from features the target has already built rather than from raw image tokens. A second limitation lies in draft-model training. Standard distillation supervises predictions along the original response trajectory, while multi-step drafting conditions on the drafter’s own proposals. Once a proposed token changes the context, the target model’s subsequent prediction may also change. Existing approaches improve supervision at such states by matching target logits or features, including states induced by the drafter (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2026). We instead supervise how the target prediction changes relative to the original trajectory. Since standard distillation already teaches the target distribution on the original context, this objective targets only what changes once the drafter’s token enters the context. 1arXiv:2610.00623v1 [cs.CV] 30 Sep 2026 Preprint Based on these observations, we propose H AWK , a multimodal speculative decoding framework that improves both the information provided to the drafter and the supervision used to train it. For target hidden states, rather than selecting layers only by depth, H AWK uses inter-layer representation similarity to identify distinct regions, selects representative layers within each region, and learns how to combine their features. For visual information, it reuses and compresses target visual hidden states computed during prefill, providing richer visual conditioning without requiring the drafter to recover the same information from raw visual tokens. During training, H AWK additionally substitutes the drafter’s own proposals into the context and trains the drafter to match the resulting shift in target predictions. Across ten multimodal benchmarks on SmolVLM-256M, H AWK consistently improves speculative drafting. Under greedy decoding, it increases average acceptance length from 3.32 to 4.08 and speedup ratio from 2.19 × to 2.60 × over EAGLE-3, while also outperforming ViSpec and HiViS on average acceptance length and speedup. Under sampling, it improves average acceptance length from 2.89 to 3.41 and speedup ratio from 1.92 × to 2.19 × over EAGLE-3. On the larger LLaVA-1.6-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B targets, H AWK raises average acceptance length under greedy decoding from 3.73 to 4.02 and from 3.19 to 3.40, and improves both acceptance length and speedup on every benchmark at both temperatures. Our contributions are twofold: • We improve multimodal drafting inputs through representation-based target-layer selection, learned feature fusion, and compressed visual hidden states reused from target prefill. • We complement standard distillation with supervision on target prediction shifts caused by the drafter’s own proposals, improving training for multi-step drafting without additional inference-time computation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Speculative decoding for language models. Speculative decoding verifies several drafted tokens in one target forward pass (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). Later methods differ in where those drafts come from. Medusa attaches extra decoding heads to the target (Cai et al., 2024). LayerSkip and Kangaroo draft by exiting or reusing the target’s own early layers, and LEAP searches over which layers to reuse (Elhoushi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Zheng & Liu, 2026). EAGLE instead trains a separate lightweight drafter that reads target hidden states, with later versions adding dynamic draft trees and features from several layers (Li et al., 2024a;b; 2025). On the training side, EAGLE-3’s training-time test feeds the drafter its own features during training (Li et al., 2025), and HASS aligns training with the drafter’s own contexts (Zhang et al., 2025); in both, the input tokens remain those of the training sequence. DistillSpec distills the target distribution on drafter-generated sequences (Zhou et al., 2024), and Draft-OPD applies on-policy distillation to speculative draft models (Lei et al., 2026). H AWK keeps the EAGLE-3 drafter and instead supervises how the target prediction changes after a draft token alters the context. 

Speculative decoding for vision-language models. Vision-language speculative decoding must provide visual context to the drafter without incurring the full cost of the target model. Early approaches construct multimodal drafters using target vision encoders or specialized multimodal training (Ganesan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025). More recent methods increasingly reuse target-model information: DREAM injects intermediate target representations, while SpecVLM conditions an EAGLE-style drafter on target features together with adaptive visual compression (Hu et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025). ViSpec learns compact visual representations for a shallow drafter, whereas HiViS avoids explicit visual-token processing by transferring target representations in which visual and textual information have already been fused (Kang et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026). H AWK also builds on target representations, and differs in how it chooses and fuses target layers and in passing pooled target visual states directly to the drafter. 

Layer similarity. Representation-similarity measures such as CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019) show that adjacent layers of deep networks are often highly similar, and layer-pruning work uses this redundancy to remove layers of LLMs (Men et al., 2024; Gromov et al., 2024). ## 3 METHOD 

HAWK changes two parts of an EAGLE-3 drafter: the target information it receives (Sections 3.1 and 3.2) and how it is trained (Section 3.3). Figure 1 summarizes both. 3.1 LAYER -R EGION FUSION 

EAGLE-3 combines hidden states from three target layers at fixed depths, (2 , N/ 2, N − 3) in 1-based indexing (Li et al., 2025). For SmolVLM-256M these are decoder blocks [1 , 14 , 26] in the 0-based indexing we use (Appendix A). Fixed positions need not match where the target representations change, and reading more layers does not necessarily help, because nearby layers are often redundant. We therefore split the target into regions by representation similarity, select a few layers in each region, and learn how to combine them.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and benchmarks. We use SmolVLM-256M-Instruct (Marafioti et al., 2025) as the main target and report LLaVA-1.6-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B in Section 4.4. We evaluate on ten bench-marks: MMMU, SEED-Bench, MME, VQAv2, and MM-Vet for general understanding; MathVista, ScienceQA, and GQA for visual reasoning; and TextVQA and DocVQA for OCR (Yue et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2026; Goyal et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024; 2022; Hudson & Manning, 2019; Singh et al., 2019; Mathew et al., 2021). Each benchmark uses 80 prompts (Appendix H). 

Baselines. We compare with EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025), trained with the same data, offline cache, and budget as H AWK , and with ViSpec (Kang et al., 2026) and HiViS (Xie et al., 2026), trained with their official implementations. All methods use the same evaluator, draft-tree configuration, and prompts (Appendix C). 

Metrics. Following HiViS, we report the speedup ratio (SR), the generation token rate relative to autoregressive decoding, and the average acceptance length τ , the number of tokens produced per verification step (Appendix B). We do not report task accuracy, since speculative decoding leaves the target’s output distribution unchanged. 

Training setup. Following HiViS, we train on 68K text-only conversations from ShareGPT and 68K multimodal conversations from LLaVA-Mix665K (ShareGPT, 2023; Liu et al., 2023), with responses regenerated by the target. The EAGLE-3 and H AWK drafters have a single decoder layer and are trained for 20 epochs (Appendix A). 

Inference setup. Following ViSpec and HiViS, we evaluate greedy decoding ( T = 0 ) and sampling (T = 1 ). All runs use one NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU with batch size 1. The draft tree has depth 5, top-10 expansion, and 60 candidate tokens (Appendix B). 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced H AWK to improve multimodal speculative decoding through better target conditioning and supervision. Layer-region fusion combines features from representation-based target regions, and visual-state pooling compresses target-processed visual states into a shorter sequence. Prediction-shift supervision teaches the drafter to match target logit shifts after plausible token substitutions. On SmolVLM-256M, H AWK improves acceptance length and speedup over EAGLE-3, ViSpec, and HiViS under both greedy decoding and sampling, and on LLaVA-1.6-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B it improves both metrics over EAGLE-3 on every benchmark at both temperatures. ## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Layer indexing. Target layers are indexed from 0 by decoder block: layer l is the output of the l-th decoder block, i.e., hidden_states[ l+1 ] in HuggingFace convention, where 

hidden_states[0] is the embedding output. SmolVLM-256M has N = 30 decoder blocks, Qwen2.5-VL-7B has N = 28 , and LLaVA-1.6-7B has N = 32 . In this indexing the EAGLE-3 rule selects blocks (1 , N/ 2 − 1, N − 4) , e.g., [1 , 14 , 26] for SmolVLM-256M; this is (2 , N/ 2, N − 3) 

in the 1-based notation of Li et al. (2025). The CKA search ranges over blocks 1 to N − 2.

Drafter. A single-layer EAGLE-3 drafter (hidden size 576, 9 attention heads, 3 KV heads, intermedi-ate size 1536) with a 32,000-token draft vocabulary mapped from the 49,280-token target vocabulary, in bfloat16. Training-time test unrolls 7 draft steps. 

Optimization. AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .999 , no weight decay), constant learning rate 10 −4

without warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0, bfloat16, seed 42, 20 epochs, on 8 NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs. 

Training data. Following HiViS (Xie et al., 2026), we combine 68,000 text-only conversations from ShareGPT with 68,000 multimodal conversations from LLaVA-Mix665K, filter out examples whose responses contain fewer than five words, and regenerate assistant responses with the target model, giving 132,929 training records. Target hidden states of the selected layers and target logits are pre-computed once and stored as an offline cache. 

Visual-state pooling. SmolVLM encodes an image as 9, 13, or 17 tiles depending on aspect ratio, with 64 image rows per tile (576, 832, or 1,088 rows; 832 for the 13-tile case quoted in the main text). Pooling acts on each contiguous run of image rows independently. With c = 4 each tile keeps 16 rows (208 for 13 tiles); the 16 × setting keeps 4 rows per tile (52 for 13 tiles). Each pooled row takes the token id and position id of the first row in its group, so text rows keep their original absolute positions and the RoPE positions seen by the drafter are a subset of those seen by the target. Within a tile, rows follow raster order over an 8 × 8 grid, so a group of four consecutive rows covers a 1 × 4

horizontal strip of patches. The EAGLE-3 baseline receives all image rows.

## B HARDWARE AND TIMING PROTOCOL 

All evaluation runs on a single NVIDIA RTX A5000 (24 GB) with the other GPUs on the machine idle, one prompt at a time (batch size 1), in bfloat16, with three warmup prompts before timing. Tree decoding uses depth 5, 60 candidate tokens, and top-k 10. Prompts are limited to 4,000 input tokens and generation to 500 new tokens. At T = 1 , the target samples from its full output distribution without top-k or nucleus truncation. Acceptance length τ is, per prompt, the mean over verification rounds of the number of accepted draft tokens plus one, averaged over prompts. Speedup ratio follows the definition of HiViS (Xie et al., 2026): per prompt, the method’s generation token rate divided by that of autoregressive decoding, averaged over prompts. Greedy results are reported on prompts whose decoded answer is identical to the autoregressive answer for every method in the table.
