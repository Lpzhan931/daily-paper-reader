## ABSTRACT 

Long-form reasoning makes inference expensive, and speculative decoding miti-gates this cost by verifying multiple draft tokens in parallel. Its speedup, how-ever, can fade as context grows and draft acceptance declines. We focus on attention-mass dilution: as softmax normalizes over more visible Keys, the mass concentrated on the highest-scoring Keys can decrease. We introduce S HARP -DRAFT , a training-free method that counteracts this effect through cardinality-aware Query scaling, without the computational overhead of online adaptation. Under explicit assumptions, we derive an exact top-k mass correction and deploy a closed-form fixed-slope approximation. Across AIME-26, GPQA-Diamond, and LongGenBench Diary, S HARP DRAFT achieves 2.59 –3.19 × geometric-mean end-to-end speedups over target-only autoregressive decoding when applied to DFlash, PARD, and EAGLE 3.1. With DFlash, it improves decoding speed and outper-forms full-parameter and LoRA-based online adaptation in end-to-end speedup, while matching the unmodified drafter’s reported peak allocated GPU memory. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have expanded their use from short question answering to long-form writing, long-horizon reasoning, agentic workflows, and multi-turn dia-logue (Bai et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025; Motwani et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026; Bai et al., 2024). These workloads either produce long outputs within a single request or repeatedly append new inter-actions to an already growing history. Autoregressive decoding remains slow in this setting because it generates only one token per sequential decoding step. Speculative decoding reduces the number of sequential target-model passes by using a lightweight draft model to propose several tokens for parallel verification (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). A successful verification can accept multiple draft tokens at once rather than producing a single token as in autoregressive decoding. The resulting speedup depends in part on the mean accepted length (MAL), reported here as the number of output tokens emitted per target verification. The benefit of speculative decoding does not remain constant throughout a long generation. MAL can decrease as generation proceeds. This increases the number of target-model verification steps required per generated token. DFlash’s Qwen3-8B draft model is trained with a maximum sequence length of 3,072 tokens (Chen et al., 2026). Figure 1(a) illustrates this behavior on LongGenBench Diary: DFlash, PARD, and EAGLE 3.1 achieve high throughput relative to AR early in generation, but their advantage declines as generation continues. Throughout this paper, EAGLE 3.1 refers to the post-norm EAGLE-3 configuration incorporating the normalization changes introduced in Attention Drift (Eldenk et al., 2026) and described in the EAGLE 3.1 release (EAGLE Team et al., 2026). OSD and OnlineSpec use target feedback to adapt the draft model during inference and improve its agreement with the target (Liu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2026). We ask whether growing context is also accompanied by a systematic change in the draft model’s attention. Softmax attention can become more diffuse as the number of visible Keys grows (Veliˇ ckovi´ c et al., 2025). Figure 1(b)

In this paper, we introduce S HARP DRAFT , a training-free method that counteracts attention-mass dilution through cardinality-aware Query scaling. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 SPECULATIVE DECODING AND ONLINE DRAFT ADAPTATION 

Speculative decoding reduces the latency of autoregressive LLM inference (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). A lightweight draft model proposes multiple tokens, which the target model verifies in parallel. The resulting speedup depends in part on how many draft tokens are accepted. Recent advances, including EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025) and DFlash (Chen et al., 2026), improve draft quality by using target-model features to guide token prediction. However, draft models trained on short sequences may not generalize well to longer contexts. LongSpec addresses this mismatch in part by introducing novel position indices for short-sequence draft training (Yang et al., 2026). Test-Time Speculation (TTS) reports that acceptance in EAGLE-3 and DFlash declines as generation proceeds, reducing their decoding speedup (Kumar et al., 2026). Online draft adaptation addresses inference-time mismatch by learning from target feedback col-lected during serving. Online Speculative Decoding (OSD) continually distills draft models on observed queries to adapt to the serving distribution (Liu et al., 2024). OnlineSpec formulates draft– target interaction as online learning and develops algorithms for updating draft models from ver-ification feedback (Qian et al., 2026). TTS adapts the draft model during generation using target supervision from successive verification rounds (Kumar et al., 2026). It uses strided updates and asynchronous execution to reduce the impact of training on decoding latency. Our work also ad-dresses declining draft acceptance during long generations, but focuses on the dilution of attention mass as the number of attended Keys grows. Rather than updating the draft model weights, S HARP -DRAFT mitigates this effect by scaling Queries according to the current Key count. 2.2 ATTENTION NORMALIZATION AND LENGTH GENERALIZATION 

Attention normalization can affect how models generalize to longer contexts. LM-Infinite shows that increasing the number of attended tokens can push attention entropy beyond its training range when 2attention logits remain bounded (Han et al., 2024). Veliˇ ckovi´ c et al. (2025) establish conditions under which softmax attention disperses as the number of attended tokens grows. They propose ad-justing the inference-time temperature based on attention entropy to mitigate this effect. Mudarisov et al. (2025) analyze how softmax scaling and the geometry of token representations constrain selec-tive attention. Scalable-Softmax mitigates attention dilution by scaling Queries logarithmically with the number of attended tokens (Nakanishi, 2025). It considers both learning the scaling parameters during pretraining and replacing softmax after pretraining. Unlike these studies, our work addresses declining draft acceptance in speculative decoding during long generations. To that end, we analyze attention normalization to distinguish the effect of Key count from changes in the score contrast between the top-k Keys and the remaining Keys. This analysis yields a Query scaling rule that preserves a reference top-k attention mass under stated assumptions. S HARP DRAFT uses a lightweight approximation of this rule, which we evaluate in terms of draft acceptance and end-to-end decoding latency.

We integrate S HARP DRAFT into DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) by rescaling queries after Q-normalization, sharing the scale across all five draft layers in our primary evaluation. The scaling affects query scores against both context and current-block keys. The target model and verification procedure are unchanged, as is the construction of persistent context keys and values from target-model features. Scale changes therefore require no rebuilding of cached context keys and values. Appendix B.1 specifies the BF16/FP32 arithmetic. At each drafting step, Nt includes visible persistent-context and current-draft-block keys, excluding masked and padded positions. Our full-attention implementation uses persistent-context length plus draft-block size and retains the count across turns when context is retained. With a fixed sliding window, the count and scale saturate. The parameter k defines the analytical partition and does not restrict which keys receive attention. The deployed rule requires no online top-k selection or reference-curve evaluation: it computes a scalar scale from the count and rescales the queries. Both operations are included in E2E latency, with no additional model forward pass, backward pass, or optimizer state. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate how S HARP DRAFT affects decoding latency, memory use, and draft acceptance across models and generation lengths. 

Evaluation setup. Our primary experiments pair Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Llama-3.1-8B with their corresponding DFlash draft models (Chen et al., 2026). We compare the unmodified draft model (Frozen), Full Adaptation, LoRA, and S HARP DRAFT , using target-only autoregressive de-coding (AR) as the latency reference. Full Adaptation updates all parameters of the draft model using target feedback during generation. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) updates only the low-rank adapter parameters, with rank 16 unless otherwise specified. Both adaptation baselines use a forward-KL distillation objective to align the draft model’s token distributions with those of the target (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). They use target distributions obtained during verification and schedule periodic updates during generation, a setting similar to that of Test-Time Speculation (TTS) (Kumar et al., 2026). S HARP DRAFT applies Equation 5 without updating model parameters. We measure end-to-end (E2E) speedup, peak allocated memory, online update cost, and mean accepted length (MAL), defined as emitted tokens per target verification. Hardware, execution backends, and mea-surement protocols are specified in Appendix B. 4.1 END -TO -E ND EFFICIENCY

We examine whether more accurate attention-mass restoration improves acceptance enough to jus-tify the cost of state-dependent calibration. Although S HARP DRAFT fixes the shared slope at s = 5 ,its Query scale still varies with visible-Key count through Equation 5. The approximation avoids measuring current attention statistics and computes the scale from cardinality alone. For a more accurate correction, Layerwise Taylor evaluates the current contrast and its derivative separately in each draft layer, re-centering the Taylor expansion near the previous applied scale. This requires additional Query–Key score computation and top-32 partitioning in all five layers at each post-reference drafting step. We compare this variant with Frozen and S HARP DRAFT on AIME-26 using Qwen3-8B/DFlash with native vLLM. Appendix C.5 gives the update rule and measurement details. Layerwise Taylor restores attention mass more accurately and yields a modest MAL gain. Relative to S HARP DRAFT , Layerwise Taylor reduces mean absolute top-32 mass error from 6.163 to 2.388 

percentage points and raises whole-request MAL from 4.545 to 4.667 (2.7% ), averaging requests equally (Figures 5b and 5c). Most of the additional MAL gain comes from capped or repetitive outputs. These results are consistent with a benefit from mitigating dilution, but do not isolate its causal effect because layerwise correction, the scale cap ( 2 rather than e0.5), and generation paths also differ. Mean per-request E2E time is 89 .70 seconds for Frozen, 59 .24 for S HARP DRAFT , and 77 .90 for Layerwise Taylor, with Taylor taking 31 .5% longer than S HARP DRAFT . Because output lengths and GPU assignments differ in this full-cohort comparison, we also assess runtime on requests with identical output tokens and termination for S HARP DRAFT and Layerwise Taylor, measuring each comparison on the same GPU. On these matched requests, Layerwise Taylor increases mean E2E time from 25 .87 to 31 .28 seconds ( 20 .9% ). These timings include calibration during generation but exclude separate attention-mass diagnostics. For this native-vLLM implementation, the extra calibration cost outweighs the acceptance gain, favoring the simpler fixed-slope rule. We separately test the choice of s = 5 by sweeping s ∈ { 3, 4, 5, 6, 8} on AIME-26, holding N0, k,and βmax fixed. The default s = 5 gives the highest observed whole-request MAL ( 4.545 ), while 

s = 4 and s = 6 remain within 1.6% (Figure 5a). A separate pooled-reference diagnostic yields effective slopes between 4.995 and 5.112 (Appendix C.4). Together, these results support s = 5

## 6 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Query scaling changes how sharply the draft model attends, but cannot by itself correct every source of disagreement with the target. For fixed Queries and Keys, positive scaling preserves score order and can reinforce an incorrect preference. The layerwise experiment illustrates this distinction: restoring attention mass more closely adds little to MAL. Learned updates may still be useful when the mismatch concerns content rather than concentration; combining selective online adaptation with Query scaling is a natural extension. The theoretical guarantee is narrower than the empirical evaluation. Our reference-inverse mass-preservation result assumes Ct ≥ Cref , agreement between current and reference contrasts through-out the application range, and inactive upper clipping. The deployed rule uses a Taylor approxi-mation and a fixed slope, whose adequacy also depends on current/reference contrast offsets (Ap-pendix C.5). Its slope and reference count should therefore be validated for a new draft model, rather than assumed to recover a universal calibration rule. Our end-to-end evidence is strongest for DFlash under greedy decoding; the transfer experiments show that the benefit can vary with the target–draft pair. The multi-turn study extends acceptance measurements to 100K generated tokens, not latency measurements, and retains repetitive contin-uations. Since repetitive text can be easy to draft, evaluating answer quality alongside latency is important before interpreting high long-context MAL as a practical gain. Whether the latency gains persist under stochastic decoding, batching, or concurrent serving remains untested. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We presented S HARP DRAFT , which scales draft Queries according to visible-Key cardinality with-out training the draft model. Separating attention-score contrast from the number of competing Keys leads to a reference-based correction, implemented through a fixed-slope approximation derived from a Taylor expansion. On the evaluated DFlash workloads, this correction yields higher end-to-end speedup than Frozen and online adaptation while matching Frozen’s peak allocated memory.
